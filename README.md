Unpaid insurance claims can create serious financial pressure for healthcare providers. Even when a medical office delivers services correctly and submits claims on time, insurance companies may deny payments because of coding discrepancies, missing documentation, eligibility concerns, or payer specific requirements. When these denials remain unresolved, accounts receivable continues to grow, collections slow down, and staff spend valuable hours investigating older balances.

Effective denial management connects claim review, payment follow up, and accounts receivable monitoring into one organized process. Instead of treating every denial as an isolated problem, billing teams can identify the reason for each rejection, determine the appropriate corrective action, and monitor progress until the account reaches a clear resolution.

For medical practices seeking faster payments, the objective is not simply to submit more claims. It is to recover legitimate reimbursement, reduce preventable denials, and establish a reliable process for handling outstanding balances. A structured approach gives billing teams better visibility into unpaid revenue and helps providers make informed decisions about their financial operations.

## **Understanding the Connection Between Denials and Accounts Receivable**

Accounts receivable represents money owed to a healthcare provider for services already delivered. These balances may include claims awaiting insurance processing, denied claims under review, partially paid claims, and amounts assigned to patients after insurance adjudication.

When a claim is denied, the expected payment may remain outstanding until the billing team corrects the problem, submits supporting documentation, or challenges an incorrect payer decision. If no one reviews the denial promptly, the balance can remain unresolved for weeks or months.

This creates more than a collection problem. Older accounts require additional research, staff may need to retrieve records from previous visits, and filing or appeal deadlines can become harder to meet. Meanwhile, the practice may struggle to estimate how much revenue will actually arrive.

A strong accounts receivable process separates unpaid claims according to their current status. Claims awaiting routine processing should not receive the same treatment as accounts with a formal denial or a payment discrepancy. Each category requires a different response.

Billing teams should also distinguish between an initial rejection and a denial issued after adjudication. A rejection may indicate that a claim failed an initial submission check, while a denial generally reflects a payer decision requiring investigation. Understanding this difference helps staff choose the correct next step instead of repeating an ineffective submission.

When accounts receivable and denial information are reviewed together, practices gain a more complete picture of outstanding revenue and can focus their attention on the accounts most likely to require intervention.

## **Establishing a Consistent Denial Review Process**

A reliable denial management workflow begins with accurate information. Every denied claim should have a recorded reason, the amount involved, the payer's response date, the relevant documentation, and a clearly defined next action. Without these details, staff may repeat previous work or overlook an important deadline.

An effective review process should follow a consistent sequence:

* Review the explanation of benefits or electronic remittance advice to understand the payer's decision.  
* Confirm patient eligibility, coverage details, authorization requirements, and claim information where relevant.  
* Identify whether the issue involves coding, documentation, timely filing, coordination of benefits, or another payer requirement.  
* Determine whether the claim needs correction, additional records, reconsideration, or a formal appeal.  
* Assign responsibility for the next action and record a follow up date.  
* Update the account when the payer responds or the claim reaches a new stage.

The purpose of this process is to move each account toward resolution. Recording a denial without assigning a corrective action does little to improve collections.

Staff should also document payer conversations carefully. Reference numbers, representative details when available, requested documents, and expected response dates can help prevent repeated inquiries. If another employee takes over the account, the notes provide a clear history of what has already happened.

A defined review process creates accountability while reducing unnecessary repetition. It also helps managers identify accounts that have stalled and need additional attention.

## **Prioritizing Older Balances to Improve Collection Speed**

Not every denied claim carries the same level of urgency. Some accounts may require a simple correction, while others involve complex documentation requirements or a disputed payment decision. A useful prioritization method considers the age of the balance, the amount at stake, the reason for denial, and any applicable filing or appeal deadline.

Claim aging reports help billing teams organize outstanding accounts into meaningful groups. Recently submitted claims may still be within normal processing periods, whereas older claims may require direct payer contact or escalation.

However, age alone should not determine priority. A smaller claim approaching a strict appeal deadline may require immediate action, while a larger claim awaiting a documented payer review may need a scheduled follow up. Staff should evaluate both financial value and the risk of losing the opportunity to collect.

A practical prioritization method includes:

* Deadline sensitive accounts: Claims approaching applicable correction, reconsideration, or appeal deadlines.  
* High value balances: Accounts with significant reimbursement amounts that could affect cash flow.  
* Quick correction opportunities: Claims with identifiable errors that can be resolved through a straightforward update.  
* Repeatedly delayed accounts: Claims with multiple payer inquiries, unresolved requests, or no meaningful progress.  
* Recurring denial patterns: Accounts that may reveal a wider issue affecting several claims.

This approach helps teams use their time more effectively. It also reduces the chance that staff will spend most of their day on easy accounts while complex or time sensitive balances remain unattended.

Practices should establish follow up schedules based on payer requirements and internal procedures. There is no single timeline suitable for every insurer or denial type. The goal is to act promptly, document each step, and avoid allowing an account to remain inactive without a clear reason.

## **Identifying the Root Causes of Repeated Denials**

Resolving an individual claim is important, but preventing similar denials is equally valuable. When the same errors appear repeatedly, the practice may be dealing with a process problem rather than a series of unrelated incidents.

Common causes include inaccurate demographic information, inactive coverage, missing authorization, incomplete documentation, coding discrepancies, incorrect modifiers, and coordination of benefits errors. Some denials may also result from payer policy requirements or differences between the submitted claim and the medical record.

The billing team should classify denial reasons and review them regularly. This allows managers to determine which problems occur most frequently, which create the greatest financial impact, and which can be addressed through workflow changes.

For example, repeated eligibility denials may indicate that coverage verification is not being completed consistently before appointments. Coding related denials may point to a need for additional review of documentation, code selection, or payer specific guidelines. If claims are denied for missing records, the office may need a more dependable process for gathering and submitting supporting information.

Corrective measures should address the source of the issue. Staff training, updated checklists, stronger registration procedures, and pre submission claim reviews can reduce errors before claims reach the payer.

It is also important to evaluate the results of these changes. If a practice introduces an authorization checklist, managers should monitor whether authorization related denials decline over subsequent reporting periods. This turns denial analysis into a practical improvement process rather than a report that receives little attention.

A consistent root cause review helps protect future revenue and reduces the administrative burden associated with repeated corrections.

## **Strengthening Follow Up and Payment Reconciliation**

A claim is not fully resolved simply because the insurer has reviewed it. The billing team must confirm the outcome, post the payment correctly, and determine whether any remaining balance requires additional action.

Some claims receive partial payment, while others are adjusted according to contractual terms. In certain situations, the payer may process a corrected claim but still leave an unexplained difference between the expected and actual reimbursement. These discrepancies should be investigated before the account is closed.

Payment reconciliation connects denial resolution with the actual financial results. Staff should compare the remittance information against the claim record, verify contractual adjustments, and identify outstanding amounts that require follow up.

When payment is lower than expected, the team should determine whether the difference reflects a valid adjustment, patient responsibility, an incorrect posting, or a possible underpayment. The appropriate response depends on the contract, payer decision, and applicable billing requirements.

Follow up documentation is essential throughout this stage. A useful account record should show the latest payer response, the amount received, any outstanding balance, and the next action required. This prevents claims from being marked complete before all relevant issues have been addressed.

Practices should also reconcile electronic payments and remittance information regularly. Delayed posting can make accounts receivable appear higher than it actually is and create unnecessary follow up work. Accurate records provide a clearer view of collection performance and help staff avoid contacting insurers about claims that have already been paid.

These procedures make it easier to distinguish genuinely unpaid balances from administrative delays. They also give practice managers greater confidence in the financial information used for planning.

## **Using Performance Reports to Guide Denial Management**

Performance reporting helps managers understand whether denial management activities are producing meaningful results. Rather than relying on general impressions, practices can evaluate trends in denial volume, outstanding balances, collection time, and recovery outcomes.

A useful reporting process should focus on measures that support practical decisions. For example, the denial rate can reveal whether a large share of submitted claims is encountering payment problems. The age of outstanding accounts can highlight delays, while the value of recovered denials can help show the financial benefit of follow up efforts.

Important measures may include:

* Denial rate: The proportion of claims denied during a defined reporting period, using a consistent calculation method.  
* Days in accounts receivable: An indicator of how long revenue generally remains outstanding.  
* Denial recovery rate: The share of denied claims or denied amounts successfully recovered, based on a clearly defined measurement.  
* Outstanding denied balance: The total value of denied claims that remain unresolved.  
* Average time to resolution: The time required to move a denied claim from identification to a final outcome.  
* Underpayment recovery: The value of identified reimbursement discrepancies that are successfully resolved.

Each measure has limitations. For example, a low denial rate does not necessarily mean that a practice collects payments quickly. Similarly, an increase in recovered revenue may reflect successful work on older accounts rather than improvement in current claim quality.

Managers should review several indicators together and compare results across consistent periods. They should also separate payer related delays from issues the practice can directly control. This provides a fairer understanding of performance and helps teams focus on the most productive improvements.
